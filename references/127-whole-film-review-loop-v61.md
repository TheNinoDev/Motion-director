# 127 — V61: Catch weak sections BEFORE the costly full render

The entire timeline is the deliverable. V60 rendered one elaborate center-section motion proof while leaving other long stretches relatively inert. A technically successful isolated test does not validate the film. This guide instructs the art director to inspect the **whole rough cut** for editorial energy, while avoiding arbitrary constant-motion quotas.

## Step 1 — Script/audio first when supplied

Identify exact existing words, voice take, music track and rights status. Preserve them. Measure durations, phrase timing and sound structure using tools available; distinguish confident observation from inferred alignment. No repeated scriptwriting or requests for assets already present. Match visual peaks to meaning and the actual music's rises, breaks and landing, with deliberate anticipations and silence. Do not cut on every detected transient or use a generic 5-second-per-feature slide deck. If music contains vocals, deliberately manage collisions with the narrator. Honor factual product status and the information-only source firewall.

## Step 2 — Design a film-level contrast map before coding

Write one compact private row per **meaningful visual event** (not per feature label): narrative purpose, current primary subject, composition silhouette, on-screen action, impact/reaction, resulting new state, connection to next and audio motivation. Lay the rows side by side; mark repeated silhouette/headline/object-position combinations. Fix repetition **now**, not after a full render. If 75% of events could be summarized as 'title + same diagram + slight glow', restart the concept at the story mechanism level. This 75% is a diagnostic illustration, not a programmatic threshold.

Use a sequence with different energy roles: something grabs attention, changes expectation, escalates, resets/reveals, has a deliberate hit/turn, resolves. The user-supplied Claude clip accomplishes this across script/question, button field, game choice, code inspection, real statistics metaphor and large green conclusion. DO NOT reproduce its graphics, typography, exact scenes, copy, source-frame imagery or soundtrack. Instead learn the alternation between macro, pattern, genuine spatial reconfiguration, readable information and decisive contrast.

## Step 3 — Prototype the weakest TWO classes, not one impressive scene

First make at least two genuine styleframes with **different compositions**, ideally opening and major payoff. Then make a short motion proof for the riskiest causal transformation and a short proof for the segment likely to become an inactive heading/card. This second test is crucial: designers naturally test their hardest technical sequence, then overlook plain still scenes. If the user only needs a narrow revision, perform only relevant testing; avoid creating mandatory extra assets for a trivial repair. Iterate weak motion paths and art direction rather than writing 400 more lines of generic layout helpers.

## Step 4 — Low-cost complete rough cut

Render a full beginning-to-end low-resolution/lightweight preview, with actual audio when available. At regular intervals collect real frames (e.g. 0.5–1.0 s spacing), and view them as a contact sheet. Mark repetitive framing, repeated upper title geometry, repeated small center objects, unmotivated empty space, shrinking proof, and long runs of near-identical frames. Then **watch the rough cut in motion** if possible, not only its contact sheet. Assess visual motion continuity, strength of accents, perceived movement energy, visual discomfort, intelligibility and whether quiet intervals are deliberate.

An OPTIONAL diagnostic: sample video at 10 fps, grayscale at 128×228, calculate average absolute pixel differences of neighboring sampled frames, then flag spans with very low change. The case study 125 recorded ~86% low-change pairs for GPT v60 versus ~61% for Claude under the same threshold (2/255). The percentages are not creative scores and cannot be treated as acceptance limits or literal dead-frame counts. Some brilliant films have long holds and some bad ones have constant noise. **Every flagged stillness span lasting roughly 1–2+ seconds that has no stated narrative purpose should be reviewed**, not automatically rejected. Also flag full-screen random motion with no meaning: a large optical difference is not proof of good choreography.

## Step 5 — Redesign the weakest section, not just polish it

For each weak segment, name the exact causal deficit: 'same document remains in same lower-third box while three headings advance' is actionable; 'needs more epic FX' is not. First alter the visual mechanism and primary subject scale, spatial depth, eye path or relationship. Then re-time animation so entry, action and landing are perceptible. Modify material/lighting only after the composition and story function work. Protect strong approved sections and existing audio. Re-render the improved region plus transitions to its neighbors, compare real frame/time evidence. A few decisive well-made changes can outperform a hundred superficial micro-animations.

## Step 6 — Final release is actual media, not metrics

Inspect final full-resolution title legibility, safe areas, clipping, playback motion and decoded audio. Where sound cannot actually be heard, say so and request the necessary real audition, don't invent review. Decode/frame checks validate technical integrity only. Confirm all imagery was authored from scratch or explicitly authorized and all product claims respect what is verified versus in development. Verify output path and duration. Report remaining unchecked rights and quality dimensions clearly. Deliver the verified artifact. Do not call the result a finished narrated ad if required audio is absent.

### Practical 'red card' prompts

- **During cut review:** “At 8s I know the story continues, but would I notice any NEW visual event with subtitles hidden?” If no, redesign cause/scale.
- **At audio peak:** “Does the visual climax occur near the true emphasized phrase/drop or is it just a new screen of text?” If the latter, re-stage.
- **At endcard:** “Is the final emotion already achieved, and is the remaining hold longer than needed for the CTA/music?” If so, compress or provide a purposeful callback.
- **At mobile size:** “Can I actually see the transformation or just read the headline?” If only text, enlarge/recompose proof.
- **For physical motion:** “Did it suddenly stop, teleport or change speed at an unrelated timing boundary?” If yes, repair envelope/path.

This is a directing and review discipline. It does not add executable validators, scripts, tools, dependencies or production gates. Existing reviews 97 and 122 remain technique references.
